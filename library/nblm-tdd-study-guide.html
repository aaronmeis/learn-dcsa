Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency (DCSA) Dual-Track Operating Model and Industrial Security Architecture: Executive Briefing Document

1. Executive Summary

The Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency (DCSA) functions as the federal government's primary security gatekeeper under the National Industrial Security Program (NISP). Operating across 167 field locations with a workforce approximately 10,000 strong, DCSA delivers over 2.6 million background investigations annually across more than 100 federal departments, while exercising industrial security oversight for 33 federal agencies, approximately 10,000 cleared companies, and 12,500 contractor facilities.

The central thesis of the DCSA operational framework is its interlocking dual-track architecture: 1. Facility Clearance (FCL): Establishing an organizational legal entity's administrative eligibility to access or store classified information. 2. Personnel Clearance (PCL): Establishing an individual's vetted eligibility for access to classified material based on investigative scope and adjudicative guidelines.

Neither track is sufficient in isolation. A cleared facility cannot operate without cleared Key Management Personnel (KMPs), and cleared individuals cannot access facility-held classified assets without an active FCL supported by a verified contractual need.

This briefing document details the conceptual, logical, physical, and integration layers of the DCSA dual-track operating model. It highlights critical policy updates—most notably the May 2025 revision to Standard Form (SF) 328, which lowered the Q7(b) reporting threshold under Question 7(b) to 15%—and defines the strict boundaries between NISP facility clearance, CMMC Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) compliance, cloud hosting accreditations, and classified system Assessment and Authorization (A&A) under the DCSA Assessment and Authorization Process Manual (DAAG) / NIST Risk Management Framework (RMF).

2. Detailed Analysis of Key Themes

Theme 1: DCSA Organizational Evolution & Mandate

DCSA was established following congressional mandates in the 2018 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) triggered by the 2015 Office of Personnel Management (OPM) data breach. The agency was formed through a multi-stage consolidation: * October 1, 2019: Consolidation of OPM’s National Background Investigations Bureau (NBIB), DoD’s Defense Security Service (DSS), and the DoD Consolidated Adjudications Facility (DoD CAF). * October 1, 2020: Transfer of background investigation IT infrastructure from OPM and DISA, the National Background Investigation System (NBIS), data systems from the Defense Manpower Data Center (DMDC), and the National Center for Credibility Assessment (NCCA) from the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA). * FY 2022 Onward: Incorporation of the DIA Sensitive Compartmented Information Facility (SCIF) accreditation mission.

DCSA operates under four primary functional domains: 1. Industrial Security / NISP: Facility eligibility oversight governed by 32 CFR Part 117 (NISPOM). 2. Personnel Vetting: Individual background investigations, adjudication via Security Executive Agent Directive 4 (SEAD 4), and Continuous Vetting (CV). 3. Information System (IS) Authorization: Oversight of contractor classified IT systems using DAAG overlaying the NIST RMF. 4. Counterintelligence (CI): Threat identification, insider threat management via the DoD Insider Threat Management and Analysis Center (DIPMAC), and industrial base outreach.

Theme 2: The Interlocking Dual-Track Model (FCL ↔ PCL)

Sponsorship & Mandatory Clocks

A commercial company or academic institution cannot self-sponsor into the NISP. An FCL pursuit must be initiated via the National Industrial Security System (NISS) by a Government Contracting Activity (GCA) or an existing currently cleared contractor contractor demonstrating a legitimate, contractual need for classified access (documented on a DD Form 254).

The FCL lifecycle enforces strict operational gates once the sponsorship welcome notice is issued: * Day 1: Sponsorship accepted in NISS; Day 1 clock begins. * Day 20 (Facility Package Gate): The company Facility Security Officer (FSO) must submit a complete facility package into NISS. This package includes: * Form DD 441 (DoD Security Agreement) * Form SF 328 (Certificate Pertaining to Foreign Interests) * Corporate legal formation documents, board minutes, and stock ledgers * Ownership structure chart * Key Management Personnel (KMP) roster * DCSA due-date (KMP Initiation Gate): All identified KMPs requiring clearance must have their investigation questionnaires (SF-86 via eApp (DISS)) submitted and fingerprint cards processed.

Processing Timeline Benchmarks

  • Overall FCL Process: Averages approximately 180 days from sponsorship acceptance to FCL issuance, assuming no unmitigated FOCI or complex business structures.
  • Personnel Security Clearance (PCL): Secret investigations average ~56 days; Top Secret (Tier 5) investigations average ~80 days.

Key Management Personnel (KMP) Determination

KMPs are defined not strictly by executive job titles, but by security-affecting authority. Any officer, director, partner, or executive who possesses direct or indirect authority to influence decisions affecting the management or performance of classified contracts must be vetted. If an executive officer is excluded from access to classified information, formal exclusion resolutions must be passed by the board and submitted in NISS.

Possessing vs. Non-Possessing Facilities

  • Non-Possessing FCL: The contractor requires access to classified information at a government site or prime contractor location, but stores and processes no classified material on its own premises. Operational training follows the CDSE IS020.CU curriculum.
  • Possessing FCL: The contractor stores, handles, or processes classified documents or operates classified IT systems at its facility. Requires physical security safeguards (approved storage containers, closed areas, perimeter controls) and an active IS authorization under DAAG.

Continuous Vetting (CV) & Vetting Updates

Periodic Reinvestigations (PRs) have been eliminated for NISP contractor personnel. Personnel are enrolled in continuous vetting, supported by automated data checks and a Personnel Vetting Questionnaire (PVQ) refresh conducted every 5 years.

Theme 3: FOCI Architecture & The May 2025 SF 328 Policy Pivot

Major SF 328 Revisions (May 2025)

Form SF 328 was formally updated on May 1, 2025, and deployed directly into NISS on May 12, 2025. The revised form introduces major structural changes: 1. Lowered Revenue Threshold (Q7b): The Q7(b) reporting threshold was lowered to 15%. Companies receiving 15% or more of their gross revenues or aggregate foreign-interest amounts from foreign sources must disclose full details. 2. Expanded Disclosure Categories: Expanded coverage beyond traditional commercial revenues to explicitly include foreign gifts, tuition, research grants, and institutional endowments (impacting university labs and research centers). 3. Foreign Employment Disclosures: Requires a mandatory Statement of Full Disclosure of Foreign Affiliations for any corporate officers or management personnel holding foreign employment or foreign consulting ties.

FOCI Mitigation Instruments

When foreign influence is identified, FCL issuance is blocked until an approved legal mitigation instrument is executed, such as: * Board Resolutions (for minor or passive foreign holdings) * Security Control Agreements (SCA) * Special Security Agreements (SSA) * Voting Trusts or Proxy Agreements (for high-level foreign ownership)

Negotiating FOCI mitigation instruments adds months to the baseline FCL timeline. Unreported material changes in ownership, cap tables, or foreign revenues post-grant represent a severe compliance failure.

Theme 4: Information System Authorization (DAAG/RMF) vs. Adjacent Regimes

A frequent architectural failure during industrial security planning is conflating adjacent cybersecurity or compliance regimes.

Regime / Standard Scope & Purpose Does it Equal an FCL? Does it Equal a Classified System ATO?
CMMC (Level 1–3) Cybersecurity framework enforcing protection of Unclassified CUI in the commercial supply chain. NO. CMMC is a CUI baseline; it provides no authority to access classified info. NO. Applies strictly to unclassified CUI environments.
FedRAMP / GCC High Cloud Service Provider (CSP) hosting accreditation for unclassified federal data. NO. Cloud hosting posture does not grant NISS sponsorship or facility clearance. NO. Commercial cloud levels do not equal a classified system ATO.
DoD Impact Levels (IL4/IL5/IL6) Cloud security technical baselines for CUI (IL4/5) and Secret (IL6) processing. NO. Authorizes hosting infrastructure capabilities, not corporate legal entity eligibility. NO. Contractor classified systems processing IL6 data must still execute DAAG + RMF.
DAAG + NIST RMF DCSA Assessment and Authorization Process Manual overlaying Risk Management Framework. N/A (Requires an existing FCL). YES. Mandatory process for contractor systems storing/processing classified data.

Primary Artifact Matrix

  • DD Form 254: Contract Security Classification Specification. Issued by the GCA or prime; establishes the legal requirement and scope for classified access.

  • SF 328: Certificate Pertaining to Foreign Interests. Standardized disclosure for FOCI analysis.
  • SF-86: Questionnaire for National Security Positions. Initiated via eApp (DISS) for individual background vetting.

Theme 6: AI Operating Boundaries & Data Safeguards

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Large Language Models (LLMs) present high security risks when applied to industrial security workflows.

Mandatory AI Rules & Constraints

  1. Assistive-Only Scope: AI tools are strictly limited to non-PII administrative tasks, such as generating blank package checklists, summarizing public handbooks, or assisting FSOs with CDSE course study guides.
  2. Hard Block on Sensitive Datasets: SF-86 personal questionnaires, KMP biographic details, PII, and raw SF 328 cap-table/financial data must never be entered into commercial, consumer, or unapproved AI platforms.
  3. No Substitution for Governance: AI cannot sponsor facilities, adjudicate personnel eligibility, or approve FOCI determinations.
  4. Tooling Inventory: There is no official DCSA-approved commercial AI software suite for automated industrial security operations.

Quote 1: DCSA Mandate and National Scope

  • Context: Highlights DCSA's wide operational scope formed by merging legacy NBIB, DSS, and DoD CAF capabilities into a single gatekeeper authority.

Quote 2: The "Chicken or the Egg" Sponsorship Dilemma

"So what I've just described is kind of a chicken or the egg scenario that many of you have already experienced. You want to compete for classified contracts but you don't have an FCL, and yet you can't get an FCL until you have a classified contract or classified work... The government will not grant an FCL unless and until the applicant has a need to access classified information."

— Steve Mapes, Director for Small Business Programs, DCSA
(Source: DON OSBP Small Business Webinar)

  • Context: Explains why companies cannot self-sponsor into the NISP. FCL processing requires an active GCA or cleared-prime sponsor verifying a contractually driven need.

Quote 3: Comprehensive Legal Scope of Foreign Control

"A US company is considered to be under FOCI whenever a foreign interest has the power direct or indirect, whether exercised or not exercised, and whether or not exercisable through ownership of the US company Securities by contractual agreements or other means to direct or decide matters affecting the management or operations of that company..."

— Dr. Bryson Reynolds, Director/FOCI Expert, DoD Office of Small Business Programs
(Source: FOCI & SCRM Webinar)

  • Context: Cites the formal 32 CFR Part 117 definition of FOCI, emphasizing that unexercised potential power held by foreign interests is legally sufficient to trigger FOCI mitigation requirements.

Quote 4: The Gray Scale of Foreign Influence

"This whole area is gray... It is not a black mark that disqualifies your application. There is an emphasis on mitigation for these risks. So it's about understanding the depth of the risk, what exactly is the concern, what type of influence or control does this risk carry..."

— Dr. Bryson Reynolds, Director/FOCI Expert, DoD Office of Small Business Programs
(Source: FOCI & SCRM Webinar)

  • Context: Clarifies that identifying foreign ties does not produce an automatic rejection. DCSA focuses on identifying risk vectors and negotiating appropriate legal mitigation instruments.

Quote 5: Document Consistency Imperative

"What we want to do is make sure that small businesses are not only familiar with the process... but also that you understand your documentation will be thoroughly reviewed and it needs to be horizontally consistent and vertically consistent. What that means is all of the documents have to say the same thing. If not, the documents will be rejected and that's going to increase the amount of time it takes for you to get your FCL."

— Zach Bernard, Industry Representative, DCSA
(Source: FOCI & SCRM Webinar)

  • Context: Identifies minor clerical errors and cross-document mismatches across SF 328, corporate charters, KMP rosters, and DD 254 forms as primary drivers of package rejections and schedule slips.

Quote 6: Commercial Scale and Speed in SbIR/STTR Vetting

"Something that is unique to AFWERX and generally the CIBER program at large... We do this at scale and speed that requires automation commercial data sets... because we're looking at thousands of applications a given year typically within a couple weeks Sprint."

— Luke Walter, AFWERX / U.S. Air Force
(Source: FOCI & SCRM Webinar)

  • Context: Outlines how commercial AI models, natural language processing, and automated web scraping datasets are deployed by DoD service branches to rapidly screen high-volume grant applications for foreign talent recruitment programs and foreign IP risk.

Quote 7: Proactive Disclosure and Risk Governance

"Bad news ages like milk... Going being proactive and discussing it is way better than hoping that nobody finds out, because that's when people find out. Nondisclosure is way more punitive than voluntary disclosure."

— Mike Hoover, Supply Chain Risk Management (SCRM) Team, HQ AFMC
(Source: FOCI & SCRM Webinar)
